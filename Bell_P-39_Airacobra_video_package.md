### B. Editorial premise and title accuracy

- **Opening question:** Why did the same fighter earn opposite verdicts? Britain gave it up after one combat operation, and the US Army pulled it from front-line fighter work. Yet nearly half of all P-39s went to the Soviet Union, where it served as a main fighter and was flown by several of the highest-scoring Allied aces.
- **Short answer from the research:** The P-39 was designed around a high-altitude requirement, but it entered production without the turbo-supercharger that requirement assumed. The June–August 1939 NACA drag study, Army and Bell decisions, and the January 1940 order to finish the service-test aircraft without turbos left an engine that lost power above about 12,000 ft. That was fatal where the enemy flew high (Pacific, Western Europe). It mattered much less on the Eastern Front, where most air combat was lower. There the Soviets used it mainly as a fighter, valued its radio and nose-mounted firepower, modified it (removing the wing guns, adding nose ballast) and flew it in the ranges where the cannon worked. The Lend-Lease supply chain (ALSIB, the Persian corridor, Arctic convoys) put roughly 4,400–4,950 aircraft into Soviet hands. Victory totals are claims and remain disputed.
- **Key takeaway for the viewer:** A weapon's reputation depends on where and how it is used. The P-39 was a narrow tool: poor in a high-altitude war, effective in a lower-altitude war.
- **Story structure used:** Channel profile §6.2 (weapon/aircraft story). The steps map to chapters as follows: (1) the reputation paradox → Hook; (2) the problem it was designed for → *Built around a gun*; (3–4) how it worked and its hidden weakness → *The turbo decision*; (5) enemy conditions exposing it → *Wrong height, wrong war*; (6) the decision that shaped the outcome → turbo decision plus Lend-Lease routing → *The long road to the front*; (7) crew and unit result → *Where it fitted* and *Tumbling*; (8) legacy and verdict → *Verdict*. General Eastern Front air war, Lend-Lease politics and ace biographies are deliberately left out; each appears only in one line where it answers the P-39 question. The P-51 video's British-engine story is not touched.
- **Is the title literally accurate?** The brief's working title "The Fighter America Wouldn't Fly: Why Half the P-39s Ever Built Went to Stalin" is **not literally accurate** on three points:
  1. "Wouldn't fly": the USAAF did fly it. It saw combat in 1942–44 in the South Pacific, New Guinea, the Aleutians and the Mediterranean, and AAF inventory peaked at 2,150 P-39s in February 1944 (C27).
  2. "Half": the real share is **nearly half**. Soviet receipts are estimated at about 4,400–4,950 against 9,558–9,588 accepted, which is 46–52%, and about 49% on the most-cited 4,719 figure (C01–C03).
  3. "Stalin": the aircraft went to the Soviet air forces under Lend-Lease protocols, not to a person. The word frames the story politically without evidence. The revised title is in L.
- **Required qualification:** Said early in the Hook: "nearly half of all P-39s built". "Withdrawn from front-line fighter duty" (0:11.5–0:18.4) is phrased as a process, not an outright refusal. The count range is explained at 4:01–4:16.
- **Simplistic or misleading explanations to avoid:** "a hidden masterpiece"; "a bad plane"; "the NACA ruined it" (responsibility is disputed, C09); "the Soviets used it as a tank-buster" (contested, C21); "the P-39 tumbled end over end" as proven fact (C22); "Pokryshkin shot down 59 planes in a P-39" as fact (C20); "American pilots hated it" as a universal verdict (C15).
- **Present-day link:** The verdict chapter shows the P-39Q at the National Museum of the US Air Force (P07). The last line returns to the opening paradox: a weapon's value depends on the war it is sent into. No separate "today" section is added.

### E. Non-negotiable edit rules

- **Hook range:** 0:00.000–0:40.000 (hook and the protected 0:00–0:40 range coincide).
- **Target mix:** real moving footage 65.6% (284.5 s) / photographs 17.1% (74.0 s) / G 17.3% (75.0 s; of which the real document G02 is 10.0 s and created G is 65.0 s). Profile band 65–80% V is met at its lower edge (see F gate).
- **Hook mix:** zero static visuals from 0:00 to 0:40. Real V = 34.0 s = 85% (≥70% required). One motion G (G01, 6.0 s; the maximum is two, each ≤6 s). Seven plans. Three original sources (V01, V02, V03), each exactly two cuts (the maximum is two per source).
- **Same source in the hook:** at most two different cuts from any original video (no user exception).
- **Photo plans:** normal 12–15 s where the content needs it; planned 10 s (P02–P07) and 14 s (P01). **Hard maximum 15 s.**
- **Combined static sequence (P + static G + document + map):** normal ≤10 s, **hard maximum 15 s**. Every P/G row in G is directly bordered by real V. The longest static run is 14.0 s (P01). No last-resort exception used.
- **Photo reuse:** each photo once by default, at most twice outside the hook in a forced case. **No photo is reused in this package.**
- **Same source outside the hook:** there is no count limit on different, non-overlapping cuts that show new content (V01 and V02 are used many times, each at a distinct shot).
- **Video repetition:** any identical, overlapping or visually equivalent video cut may appear **only once** across hook, body and close. No second-use exception exists, and the old 24 s replay allowance no longer applies.
- **G repetition:** each unique G may appear once, in one continuous showing. No second showing after it leaves the screen.
- **Recoding does not reset the count:** crop, zoom, recolour, text or motion change, or a new G code, does not make a new asset. Visually equivalent G are audited together (see H).
- **Forced photo reuse conditions (not used here):** different chapter, ≥90 s gap, ≥8 different plans in between, `FORCED REUSE 2/2` note, total ≤18 s.
- **Third use, adjacent repeat and A–B–A are forbidden** for all assets, G included.
- **Natural speed only:** no slow motion, freeze-frame, optical flow, frame interpolation or frame repeat. Timeline duration equals source duration for every V row.
- **Template lock:** no section, table, column, row type or edit-map column may be added to or removed from the A–O structure.
- **First payoff:** 0:18.359–0:24.359 (G01, "nearly half"). Evidence: 0:24.359–0:32.325 (Soviet P-39s). The bridge to the mechanism runs 0:32.325–0:40.000, and chapter 2 opens on the mechanism at 0:40.000.
- **Breathing moments:** short break tags only: 1.0 s at about 0:35 (VO01), 1.0 s at the end of VO03 (about 2:31.8) and 1.5 s at about 6:41 (VO08). Total 3.5 s. **No visual holds longer than 3.0 s**, and all source audio is muted (see K).
- **Breaks are placed** only after the paradox line, the disputed-responsibility line and the verdict question. They are not added at every sentence end.
- **Hook functions (profile §6.1):** the strongest real footage of the carrier (P-39 nose and cannon; Soviet P-39s), the paradox, and a first clue (the 1939 decision). Hook: 81 words, about 122 wpm across 40 s (dense lines at 140–150 wpm, allowed as the more energetic hook). Body: 115–128 wpm.
- **Context labels:** every row that shows US training-film aircraft while the VO speaks of RAF, Guadalcanal or Soviet use says "Context" in the edit cell. P-63 rows carry a permanent on-screen `P-63 Kingcobra` label.

### N. Research notes and uncertainties

- **Disputed numbers:**
  - Production: 9,588 (AAF Statistical Digest Table 74, including experimental aircraft) against 9,558 (Craven & Cate Vol. VI), with Wikipedia also 9,588 in the infobox. VO: "more than 9,500".
  - Deliveries to the USSR: 4,719 and 4,773 (both in Wikipedia), 4,423–4,750 (migflug), "nearly 5,000" (historyofwar). The 1945 State Department "Report on War Aid Furnished by the United States to the U.S.S.R." was located as a catalogue record but could not be opened (DPLA blocks automated access); it should be consulted before any exact figure is used. VO: a range.
  - British Airacobras sent north: 212 dispatched, 54 lost (historyofwar); Soviet sources give lower losses; not used in the VO.
  - Pokryshkin: 59 (official) against 46 + 6 shared (Bykov, via secondary reports). The share scored in P-39s (47 or 48) conflicts and is not used.
  - P-400 numbers: 200 against 128. Not used.
- **Conflicting historical accounts:**
  - XP-39 first flight: 6 Apr 1938 (Wikipedia) against "April 1939" / "spring 1939" (Chambers; Hansen describes spring 1939 Wright Field performance tests). No date is used.
  - Production-weight comparison: "about a ton" (Hansen) against 6,150 lb in the NACA report (Chambers). VO: "far lighter".
  - Responsibility for dropping the turbo: NACA, Army or Bell (Chambers; Wikipedia, which cites Larry Bell's cash-flow motive as a hypothesis). Presented as open.
  - Soviet use: US institutional histories (Chambers: "low-level anti-tank weapons"; Craven & Cate: "low-level support of ground troops") against Soviet pilot and unit-based accounts (Golodnikov; Loza/Gebhardt via Wikipedia: primarily air combat). VO attributes the view to Soviet pilots.
  - RAF re-equipment date: December 1941 (IWM caption) against March 1942 (Wikipedia). Not used.
  - Bell film (1943) says stalls give "plenty of warning"; NACA L-602 (1943) found "no warning". Both are presented, with the NACA test as the independent measurement.
  - Tumbling: Wikipedia cites Bell's failed attempts to reproduce it, Bob Hoover's memoir and a 1970s NASA spin-tunnel re-test. That study could not be found in NTRS or in *Modeling Flight* (NASA SP-2010-3107), so the tumbling mechanism is not asserted.
- **Name, model, variant, unit and place changes:** P-400 = export P-39 (Bell Model 14) from the British order. "Caribou" was the brief British name, later Airacobra I. The P-63 Kingcobra is a different aircraft and is labelled wherever shown. The Soviet P-39s in V03 are not identified by unit; the Guards badge on "48" is visible but not read. Golodnikov's unit: 2nd Guards Mixed Aviation Regiment, Northern Fleet Air Force (as given in the interview).
- **Details that could not be verified exactly:**
  - The exact Frantic base in V03 (Poltava, Mirgorod or Piryatin) and the exact date within June 1944.
  - The exact sub-types in V01 (the narration says "Model N"), V02 (early production) and V03.
  - The shoot date of V04 (released 1945).
  - Whether the V03 reel contains any non-AAF material. The shot list and the reel's unedited character indicate AAF coverage; a final check of NARA's full shot-card is recommended.
  - The V01/V02 copyright non-renewal rests on searches of OCR text of the CCE renewal volumes 1969–1974 for the titles, "Bell Aircraft", "Bell Aerospace" and "Textron". OCR can miss entries; a Copyright Office search (Virtual Card Catalog 1955–1977) before publication is advised as a final safeguard.
- **Inflation or currency conversion:** none used.
- **Cautious wording used in the script:** "nearly half"; "estimates … run from about 4,400 to nearly 5,000"; "about 2,600"; "about 12,000 feet"; "far lighter"; "pointed to"; "Who pushed hardest is still argued"; "victory markings"; "Soviet records credit … one archival study counts"; "Soviet pilots describe"; "That is one pilot's memory"; "the famous flat-spin test".
- **Context footage not of the event itself:** V01/V02 are US training films (US fields, 1942–43). They are used, labelled "Context", in the RAF, Guadalcanal and Soviet chapters (rows at 2:41.868, 2:57.385, 3:17.825, 3:25.500, 4:52.276–5:19.733, 5:48.486). V04 shows P-63s at Ladd Field, labelled P-63. P06 is a staged firing-test photo, labelled. P07 is a modern museum photo, labelled.
