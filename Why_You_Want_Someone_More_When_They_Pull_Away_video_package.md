### B. Editorial premise and title accuracy

- **Opening question:** When someone pulls away, why does the pull toward them get stronger? Is that a sign of loving them more, or the brain reacting to uncertainty, loss and restriction?
- **Short answer from the research:** The intensity is usually not a measure of love. Several mechanisms point at the same person. Uncertainty keeps them on your mind: one small experiment found more attraction under uncertainty, but replications are mixed. Unpredictable reward is “sticky”: variable schedules sustain behaviour, and dopamine neurons signal surprise more than reward itself. Distance can feel like a freedom being removed (reactance and scarcity). For people high in attachment anxiety, distance activates the attachment system more strongly. These raise *wanting* (incentive salience) more than *liking*. When the hard-to-get effect works at all, it raises wanting while lowering liking, and mainly in people already committed (Dai et al. 2014).
- **Key takeaway for the viewer:** The strength of the craving says little about whether this is the right person. Ask instead whether you enjoy the person, or mainly the relief when they come back.
- **Story structure used:** Profile §7.3 relationship-psychology structure. (1) Familiar moment → Hook. (2) “Why is it so strong?” → *The uncertainty effect*. (3) First mechanism → uncertainty, then reinforcement in *The slot-machine schedule*. (4) The core distinction → *Wanting is not liking*. (5) The added pressure of loss → *When the option is taken away*. (6) Why some feel it more → attachment. (7) The extreme case → *Rejection in the brain*. (8) How two people feed the cycle → *The loop between two people*. (9) Clear verdict plus three self-awareness questions → *What the pull really is*. Each chapter follows the core chain: relatable problem → mechanism → example → consequence → takeaway (profile §1). **Left out:** pop “dark psychology” tactics, “trauma bonding”, attachment typing quizzes, ghosting statistics and the ethics of dating apps. None of these answers the central question with verified evidence (see D, REMOVE rows).
- **Is the title literally accurate?** Yes, with qualification. “You want someone more” is the felt experience the video explains; it does not claim that everyone feels it. The video is careful not to claim that distance *causes* more attraction in general: the uncertainty evidence is mixed (C04), and hard to get lowers liking (C10). The title describes the experience; the script explains it and states its limits.
- **Required qualification:** Said within the first minute: “Research suggests something less flattering… In one experiment…”. That is a single experiment, not a law. At 1:40.116–2:08.401 the video states the study’s size and limits and the mixed replications before going further.
- **Simplistic or misleading explanations to avoid:** “Playing hard to get works”; “science proves people want what they can’t have”; “you’re addicted to them / it’s a dopamine addiction”; “it’s a trauma bond”; “you have an anxious attachment style” as a diagnosis; “this feeling means they’re the one”; “brain scans prove love is a drug”.
- **Present-day link:** The whole video is set in present-day messaging behaviour (late replies, read receipts, scrolling at night). The close returns to the opening phone scene with a new meaning: the phone left glowing on the bedside table. No separate “today” section.

### N. Research notes and uncertainties

- **Disputed figures and findings:** (1) The uncertainty-attraction effect (C02) is a single study of 47 women; the key contrast is p = .04, and later work is mixed (C04). Montoya et al. 2015 found certainty → more liking in one paradigm and uncertainty → more attraction (via a wish to reduce uncertainty) in another; Birnbaum et al. 2018 found uncertainty lowered desirability in online chats; an Ohio University thesis did not replicate a self-relevant version. The script therefore says “one experiment” and “mixed results”. (2) Whitchurch et al.’s mediation by thought frequency is tentative by the authors’ own statement (bootstrap CI includes zero). (3) Fisher et al. 2010 reports several analyses at uncorrected thresholds with N = 15.
- **Conflicting narratives:** Popular accounts say “playing hard to get works”. Walster et al. (five null experiments), Dai et al. (lowers liking; backfires without commitment) and Birnbaum et al. contradict this as general advice. The video sides with the published evidence and avoids teaching the tactic.
- **Name and term changes:** Elaine Walster later published as Elaine Hatfield; the VO uses the 1973 byline. “Frustration attraction” is Fisher’s term (Why We Love, 2004; Fisher 2014 chapter); it does not appear in the 2010 paper and is framed as her proposal. “Incentive salience” is Berridge & Robinson’s technical term for “wanting”.
- **Details that could not be fully verified:** Exact per-condition means for Dai et al. 2014 and Worchel et al. 1975 were not extracted (paywalled), so G08 and G11 show direction or rank only. Per-group sizes in Whitchurch et al. are not shown. The exact Hazan & Shaver percentages vary across summaries, so they are not used (C27). The Birnbaum release figure (3.15 vs 2.73) is taken from the University of Rochester summary, not the full paper.
- **Inflation or currency conversion method:** Not applicable (no monetary values).
- **Cautious wording used in the script:** “Research suggests”; “in one experiment”; “the researchers’ interpretation”; “other teams have found mixed results”; “tends to be harder to extinguish”; “in a way”; “helps explain… doesn’t show”; “mostly with rats”; “tend to want it more”; “can change with experience”; “she proposes”; “it suggests… it doesn’t show”; “linked to”; “isn’t a reliable measure of love”.
- **Contextual footage that does not show the actual event:** Every human scene is a staged Pexels stock scene with performers, used as illustration. The pigeons (V20, V21) are street/park birds, not experimental animals. The rats (V29, V33) are not lab animals. The slot machines (V23, V24), MRI scanner (V56) and cookies (V45) are modern context footage. P01 shows rat operant chambers at the University of Brasília (c. 2001), not Ferster & Skinner’s pigeon apparatus. G graphics are explanatory schematics; G15 is anatomical, not study imagery.
- **Visual research dead ends (recorded per master §10.5):** (a) B. F. Skinner Foundation films on Internet Archive (“Behavior Theory in Practice” 1965; “B.F. Skinner Demonstrates Operant Conditioning” 1971; “Learning” 1971, CRM) were rejected: works from 1964 onward received automatic copyright renewal, and no item-level public-domain or open licence is stated. (b) Commons photos “B.F. Skinner at Harvard circa 1950.jpg” (CC BY 3.0 claimed by an uploader who cannot be the 1950 photographer: uploader–rights-holder doubt) and “B.F. Skinner and Robert Epstein.jpg” (CC BY-SA 4.0, a forbidden share-alike licence) were rejected. Other Skinner-box photos (“Skinner box photo 02.jpg”, “Skinner’s box.jpg”) are CC BY-SA and were rejected. (c) No rights-cleared footage of any cited experiment exists, so experiments are shown only through G graphics. (d) Pexels search and asset pages were read through a fetch tool; video files came from the Pexels CDN at the HD resolutions listed in F.
