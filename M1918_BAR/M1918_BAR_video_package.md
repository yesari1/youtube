## B. Editorial premise and title accuracy

- **Opening question:** How did an automatic rifle designed in 1917, to be fired from the hip while walking toward the enemy, become the weapon the Marine rifle squad was built around? And why did soldiers in Korea still call it indispensable, despite its flaws?
- **Short answer from the research:** Browning's M1918 was built for the French "walking fire" doctrine. It was a one-man automatic rifle with a 20-round magazine. Between the wars the Army had no portable light machine gun, so in 1938 it turned the BAR into one (the M1918A2: bipod, rate reducer, no semi-automatic setting, about 20 lb with sling). That made it the only automatic weapon in the 12-man Army squad, which it supported as a base of fire. The Marines went further. Their 1944 F-series squad had 13 men in three four-man fire teams, each built around a BAR, so the riflemen protected and fed the gun. The BAR had real limits: a small magazine, a fixed barrel, weight, and parts that needed care. Even so, S.L.A. Marshall's 1951 Army study found it was the fire base of the infantry fight in Korea. He recommended doubling the number of BARs and gave the weapon "a clean bill".
- **Core viewer takeaway:** The BAR was a poor light machine gun but an excellent one-man automatic rifle. The Marine Corps organised its squads around exactly that strength, and in 2010 it returned to the same idea with the M27.
- **Structure used:** Weapon story (profile §6.2). It runs: strong opening paradox → why it was created (1917 France, walking fire) → mechanism (gas operation, 1925 film) → first combat → how its role changed (1938 A2) → how squads used it (Army base of fire, then the Marine fire team) → its price (weight, fixed barrel, maintenance) → the test (Korea, Marshall) → verdict and legacy (M14/M60, M27).
- **Is the title literally accurate?** Yes. The USMC Table of Organization F-series (approved 27 March 1944) gives every rifle squad three fire teams, each with one automatic rifleman carrying the BAR. Webb (1972) and the Wikipedia BAR article both describe Marine doctrine as centred on the BAR.
- **Required qualification:** "Every squad" means every rifle squad on the official table of organization. Field strength varied. The script says "by 1944" and "on paper" where it gives counts.
- **Simplistic or misleading explanations to avoid:** "The BAR was designed as a light machine gun" (it was not; that role came in 1938); "every fourth German infantryman had an automatic weapon" (anecdotal, removed); "over 500,000 BARs were made" (it mixes conversions and new manufacture; removed); "Bonnie and Clyde made it famous" (off-topic; removed); "it was a failure because it could not change barrels" (only true against a light machine-gun standard).
- **Present-day connection:** In 2010 the Marine Corps picked the M27 Infantry Automatic Rifle, a magazine-fed automatic rifle for the automatic rifleman in each fire team, to replace part of its belt-fed M249s. It is the same one-man automatic rifle idea. The video makes no product or performance claims about the M27.

## E. Non-negotiable edit rules

1. **Hook:** 0:00.0–0:40.4. Zero stills and zero G in 0:00–0:40. The first still is P01 at 1:04.4 and the first G is G01 at 2:53.1. The hook is 100% real V: 6 shots from 4 original sources (V01, V02, V03, V04). No motion G in the hook. Hook word target: 91 words at ≈140 WPM.
2. **Hook source limit:** maximum two different cuts per original video in the hook. Used: V02 ×1, V01 ×2, V03 ×2, V04 ×1. No user exception.
3. **Mix target:** ≈70% real moving archive / ≈19% original G / ≈11% photographs.
4. **Photo durations:** 12–15 s (P03 15 s is the longest; absolute maximum 15 s). Each photo is used once and carries a slow, natural push or pan only.
5. **Static sequences:** photo, still G, document and map count together: normal 10 s, maximum 15 s. Motion G (G01–G06) is excluded from the static count, following the G-graphic standard (15–25 s on screen, fully built by ~8–10 s). Every P and G is isolated between V shots, so no two statics touch.
6. **Photos outside the hook:** default one use. No forced reuse in this package.
7. **Same-source cut limit:** "max two cuts per original video" applies only to the hook. Outside the hook, multiple non-overlapping cuts from one source are allowed when each shows visibly new content. V01, V05 and V07 rely on this.
8. **Video reuse:** any identical, overlapping or visually equivalent video range is used once across hook, body and closing combined. V01 10:04–10:19 (a near-duplicate prone-fire angle) is deliberately left unused next to 09:34.8–09:59.9.
9. **G use:** each unique G is shown once, in one uninterrupted appearance.
10. **Third use, adjacent repeat and A–B–A repeat** are forbidden for every asset type.
11. **Natural speed only:** no slow motion, freeze frames, optical flow, frame interpolation or frame repetition. Every V row's timeline duration equals its source duration.
12. **Template lock:** no section, table, column, row type or edit-map structure may be added or removed.
13. **First payoff / retention bridge:** the first concrete answer comes at 0:06.1–0:14.1 (why it exists: walking fire); the first visual payoff is the gas-piston animation at 1:16.4; the first data payoff is G01 at 2:53.1.

15. **Breathing rule:** breathing moments are used only for a strong image, a graphic read or meaningful archive sound, never at every sentence end.
16. **Archive audio:** all archive audio is muted. V01 is a silent film; the V03, V04, V07 and V10 narration tracks are not used.
17. **Context labels:** V07 (*The Battle of San Pietro*, partly reconstructed) is CONTEXT in every row and is never described as a BAR team in action. ## N. Research notes and uncertainties

- **First combat date:** Wikipedia gives 13 September 1918 for the 79th Division's first use. Other accounts give 22 September or the opening of the Meuse-Argonne (26 Sept). The script says "in September 1918" only.
- **"52,000 built" vs "available":** Wikipedia says 52,000 were "available" by the armistice. Production records add up to a similar figure. The VO says "built", which is acceptable for this level of detail; change it to "available" if a stricter reading is wanted.
- **Production totals:** 102,174 (M1918), 188,380 (new WWII), 61,000 (Korea) are new manufacture only. Totals of over 500,000 (e.g., American Rifleman) include conversions and rebuilds and are not used (C37).
- **F-series date:** The HyperWar transcription shows approval on 27 March 1944. Other sources date the fire-team squad to January 1944 (Webb, Marine Corps Gazette, 1972). The script says "in early 1944" and "by 1944".
- **Marshall's material:** ORO-R-13 is based on interviews, and its "five or six riflemen" and "double the BARs" points are reported attitudes, so the script attributes them ("he wrote", "he found", "what the troops asked for").
- **Hackworth:** The quote comes from his autobiography *About Face* (1989), as cited in the Wikipedia BAR article. It is attributed and short.
- **Val Browning photo:** The Commons record date (19 Feb 1918) may be a cataloguing date. The VO says only "seen here in 1918".
- **San Pietro:** John Huston's film contains scenes staged after the battle. Every V07 row is labelled CONTEXT and the VO never says the shots show a BAR team.
- **Photo captions:** P03's original caption contains a period slur, so the on-screen caption is neutral. No other caption is affected.
- **Timecode basis:** V01, V03, V09 and V10 timecodes were measured on the only available `.mp4`. V02, V05, V06 and V08 were measured on the `.mp4` derivatives of the `.mov` masters, and V04 and V07 on the `_512kb.mp4` derivatives of the `.mpeg` masters. Re-check the first frame of each range on the master.
