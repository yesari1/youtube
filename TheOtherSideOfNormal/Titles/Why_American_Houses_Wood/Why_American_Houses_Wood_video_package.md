## B. Editorial premise and title accuracy

- **Opening question:** Why does a new American house start as a pile of two-by-fours while a new German or English house starts as a brick-and-block wall — and is the wooden house really the 'flimsy' shortcut it is accused of being?
- **Short answer from the research:** American houses are wooden because, for two centuries, the US housing system — abundant forest per person, cheap sawn lumber and nails, light framing methods (balloon, then platform framing), federally insured mortgages, mass suburban building and prescriptive codes — made wood the fastest and cheapest way to build. It is not evidence that Americans value durability less: timber houses can last centuries if kept dry, but they pay their bill in moisture, termite, storm and sound protection. Much of Europe built in masonry because forests per person were far smaller and depleted early, cities wrote fire rules into law, and war-time destruction was rebuilt in brick; masonry pays in weight, time and slower building. Europe is not uniform: Sweden, Finland and Scotland build mainly in timber frame, and Germany's wood share is rising through factory-built houses.
- **Core idea the viewer leaves with:** Neither side built the wrong house; each built the right house for the system it already had — and that system is locked in by supply chains, skills and rules, not by taste.
- **Topic structure used:** Contrast → mechanism → reversal → exceptions → time lock → verdict (Profile §7.1). In scope: new single-family/low-rise housing in the US, Germany and the UK, with France, Sweden and Finland as named comparison points. Out of scope: apartment-tower construction, cross-laminated timber high-rises, interior layouts, heating systems (already covered by the channel), and a general European average.
- **Is the title literally accurate?:** The working title 'Why American Houses Are Made of Wood — But European Homes Aren't' is not literally accurate: Sweden, Finland and Scotland build mostly in timber frame. Adding 'Most' makes it accurate (Germany ≈79 %, England ≈85 % and France ≈93 % of new housing is not built mainly in wood, by their national measures). The main title in L therefore uses 'Most European Homes'. The video names the exceptions at 5:23.
- **Necessary qualification:** 'Made of wood' means the load-bearing frame (studs, joists, rafters); the outside may be siding, brick veneer or stucco. 'European' refers to Germany, England and France as the representative cases; the Nordic countries and Scotland are explicit exceptions. US statistics are for new single-family homes; German statistics are approved new residential buildings, so cross-country figures are labelled with their own scope.
- **Simple or misleading explanations to avoid:** 'Americans don't care about quality'; 'wood houses are cheap and temporary'; 'Europe has no trees' (Germany and France are about one-third forest — the difference is forest per person and history); 'it's just culture/tradition'; 'brick is always stronger' (wood frames perform differently, not worse, in many hazards); treating 'Europe' as one country.
- **Present-day link:** The lock-in is visible in current data: 94 % of new US single-family homes completed in 2025 were wood-framed, while Germany's timber share is rising mainly through prefabricated houses. The verdict ties the 2025 numbers back to the systems that produced them.
